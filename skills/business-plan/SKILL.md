Use this to write a business plan or a feasibility study.
1. Start with the reader and the decision: a bank, an investor, a partner, or the owner deciding whether to start. Lenders care most about cash flow and repayment; investors about growth and returns.
2. Structure: executive summary (written last, one page) → the business (offer, legal form, location, founders) → market (customers, size, trends, competitors) → value proposition and difference → marketing and sales (channels, pricing, acquisition cost) → operations (suppliers, equipment, staff, licences, process) → team → financial plan → risks and mitigations → funding request and use of funds.
3. Build the financials from assumptions the reader can check: start-up costs (equipment, fit-out, deposits, licences, opening stock, launch marketing), monthly fixed costs (rent, salaries, utilities, insurance, software), variable cost per sale, prices, and a month-by-month sales ramp.
4. Outputs: a 12-month cash flow forecast (above all, when cash runs lowest), a three-year profit and loss summary, the break-even point (fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per unit), the payback period, and the funding needed including a buffer of three to six months of fixed costs.
5. Scenarios: base, pessimistic (sales 30% lower, costs 10% higher) and optimistic; show the business survives the pessimistic case or what would be cut.
6. Feasibility verdict: evidence of demand, competitive position, operational practicality, financial viability, legal requirements and key risks. Say plainly when the numbers do not work.
7. Local data matters: rents, wages, prices and rules differ by city; mark assumptions to verify.
Answer shape: the plan with headings, a financial table with labelled assumptions, and a verdict naming the three biggest risks.

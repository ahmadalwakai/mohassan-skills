Use this to evaluate arguments, claims and reasoning.
1. Reconstruct the argument: the conclusion, the premises, and the unstated assumptions it needs. Many disagreements dissolve once the hidden assumption is named.
2. Check the evidence: is it relevant, sufficient and reliable? Anecdotes, single studies and quotes out of context are weak; consistent patterns across reliable sources are strong.
3. Check the logic: do the premises actually lead to the conclusion? Common fallacies: ad hominem (attacking the person), straw man (distorting the claim), false dilemma (only two options offered), slippery slope (an unjustified chain of consequences), appeal to authority outside its field, appeal to popularity, hasty generalization, post hoc (after, therefore because of), circular reasoning, red herring, moving the goalposts.
4. Watch cognitive biases, including your own: confirmation bias, anchoring, availability (judging by what is memorable), survivorship bias, sunk cost, overconfidence.
5. Steelman the other side: state its strongest form before judging it.
6. Calibrate confidence: separate proven, likely, possible and unknown, and update when the evidence changes.
7. Point out flaws kindly and precisely, with a short example of how the argument could be repaired.
Answer shape: the argument laid out, the verdict (sound, weak or fallacious), each problem named with a one-line explanation, and the evidence that would settle the question.

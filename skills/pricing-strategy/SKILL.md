Use this to set, review or change prices.
1. Know the floor: full cost per unit or job (materials, labour at a real hourly cost, travel, fees, a share of overheads) plus a target margin. Never price below the cost of delivery.
2. Know the ceiling: the value to the customer (time saved, risk avoided, money earned) and the cost of their alternatives, including doing it themselves or hiring a competitor.
3. Set the price between them by positioning: premium (best quality, fastest, guaranteed), mid-market or budget. Price itself signals quality.
4. Freelancers and services: income target ÷ realistic billable hours (often 50 to 70% of working hours) gives a minimum hourly rate; prefer project or package prices for defined work, with clear scope and revision limits.
5. Packages: three tiers (basic, standard, premium) with the middle designed as the best value and the premium tier as the anchor; few choices.
6. Discounts need a reason (volume, prepayment, off-peak, loyalty) and an end date; adding value beats cutting price. Watch the margin: a 10% discount on a 30% margin needs about 50% more sales to keep the same profit.
7. Raising prices: give a short reason (costs, improvements), give notice (for example 30 days), hold loyal customers on the old price for a while if possible, start with new customers, and monitor churn.
8. Test prices on new offers or segments, tracking conversion, average order value and margin rather than volume alone.
Answer shape: the recommended price or range with the calculation behind it, the positioning reason, and how to present it to customers.
